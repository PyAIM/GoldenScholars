    Era I — Foundations (750–900)¶

    The first 150 years of the tradition: the translation of Greek and Indian mathematics into Arabic in Abbasid Baghdad, and the first original masterpieces built on that foundation. Dates for this period are frequently approximate.

    al-Fazari¶

    Ibrahim al-Fazari and his son Muhammad al-Fazari (8th century, d. c. 796–806) · Baghdad · astronomy, instruments

    The Fazaris stand at the threshold of the tradition. When an Indian embassy brought Sanskrit astronomical material to the court of the caliph al-Mansur in the 770s, Muhammad al-Fazari participated in rendering it into Arabic as the Zij al-Sindhind, the channel through which Indian astronomy and, crucially, the decimal positional numeral system entered the Islamic world. He is also traditionally credited as among the first in the Islamic world to construct astrolabes. The biographical record for both father and son is thin and partly conflated in medieval sources; historians flag most personal details as uncertain.

    Sources: DSB (D. Pingree, "al-Fazari"); Katz, ch. on Islamic mathematics.

    al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar¶

    al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar (fl. c. 786–833) · Baghdad · translation

    The first known translator of Euclid's Elements into Arabic, producing one version under Harun al-Rashid and a revised version under al-Ma'mun. He also translated Ptolemy's Almagest. His translations made the axiomatic-deductive method of Greek geometry available to the Arabic-reading world, and the Elements he transmitted became the universal geometry textbook of the tradition. Portions of the Latin Euclid that medieval Europe used descend, through later Arabic recensions, from the Hajjaj line of transmission.

    Sources: DSB; Berggren, Episodes, ch. 1.

    Hunayn ibn Ishaq and Ishaq ibn Hunayn¶

    Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873) and Ishaq ibn Hunayn (c. 830–910/911) · Baghdad · translation

    Hunayn, a Nestorian Christian physician, headed the most productive translation workshop of the ninth century and set new philological standards, collating multiple Greek manuscripts before translating. While his own output centered on medicine, his son Ishaq produced the translations of Euclid's Elements and Ptolemy's Almagest that, as revised by Thabit ibn Qurra, became the standard mathematical texts of the Islamic world. The Ishaq–Thabit Elements is the version most Islamic geometers actually read.

    Sources: DSB; Gutas, Greek Thought, Arabic Culture.

    al-Khwarizmi¶

    Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (c. 780 – c. 850) · Baghdad (House of Wisdom) · algebra, arithmetic, astronomy, geography

    The most influential mathematician of the era and arguably of the entire tradition. Two works define his legacy:

    The Algebra. Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing," c. 820s) is the founding document of algebra as an independent discipline. Al-Khwarizmi classified equations of degree at most two into six standard types (in modern notation: \(ax^2 = bx\), \(ax^2 = c\), \(bx = c\), \(ax^2 + bx = c\), \(ax^2 + c = bx\), \(ax^2 = bx + c\), all with positive coefficients), gave systematic solution procedures for each, and supplied geometric justifications in the style of completing the square, literally, with squares. The operations al-jabr (restoring, moving a subtracted term across the equation) and al-muqabala (balancing, cancelling like terms) gave the discipline its name: algebra. The book's second half applies the method to inheritance problems, mensuration, and commerce; it is a manual for solving problems, not a commentary on Greek geometry, and this problem-solving orientation is precisely what was new.

    The Arithmetic. His treatise on reckoning with Hindu numerals survives only in Latin versions (the tradition beginning Dixit Algorizmi, "Thus spoke al-Khwarizmi"). It taught place-value notation, the zero, and the algorithms for addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and root extraction. The Latinization of his name, Algorismi, became the medieval word algorism (decimal computation) and ultimately the modern word algorithm.

    He also compiled the Zij al-Sindhind astronomical tables (the first major original zij of the tradition, blending Indian, Persian, and Greek methods) and a Book of the Depiction of the Earth that corrected Ptolemy's geography.

    Transmission. Robert of Chester (1145, Segovia) and Gerard of Cremona (Toledo, later 12th c.) translated the Algebra into Latin; it became Europe's introduction to the subject and was still cited by Italian algebraists in the 1500s. The arithmetic, through the algorismus literature, taught Europe decimal computation.

    Sources: Rashed, The Development of Arabic Mathematics; Berggren, Episodes, ch. 4; Katz; DSB (G. Toomer, "al-Khwarizmi"). English translation of the Algebra: F. Rosen (1831), superseded by Rashed's edition (2009).

    al-Jawhari¶

    al-Abbas ibn Said al-Jawhari (fl. c. 830) · Baghdad · geometry

    A geometer of al-Ma'mun's circle who participated in the caliph's astronomical observations and wrote a commentary on Euclid's Elements, notable for containing one of the earliest recorded attempts in the Islamic world to prove Euclid's parallel postulate. The attempt fails (as all such attempts must), but it opens a research line, criticism of the parallel postulate, that runs through Thabit ibn Qurra, Ibn al-Haytham, Omar Khayyam, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, and which fed, via the 1594 Rome printing of a Tusi-attributed recension and its discussion by John Wallis and Girolamo Saccheri, into the prehistory of non-Euclidean geometry.

    Sources: DSB; Rosenfeld, A History of Non-Euclidean Geometry.

    al-Kindi¶

    Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (c. 801–873) · Baghdad · philosophy, mathematics, optics, cryptanalysis

    "The Philosopher of the Arabs" wrote across an enormous range; his mathematical importance is threefold. First, his works on geometrical optics transmitted and corrected the Euclidean theory of vision and influenced Ibn al-Haytham. Second, his treatise On Deciphering Cryptographic Messages contains the earliest known description of frequency analysis, the statistical method for breaking substitution ciphers; this is the founding document of cryptanalysis and an early landmark of statistical reasoning, a point of direct relevance to modern cybersecurity curricula. Third, his numerous treatises on arithmetic helped popularize Indian numerals among the educated public.

    Sources: Rashed (ed.), works on al-Kindi's optics; Ibrahim Al-Kadi, "Origins of Cryptology: The Arab Contributions," Cryptologia 16 (1992); Simon Singh, The Code Book (popular account).

    The Banu Musa¶

    Muhammad, Ahmad, and al-Hasan, the "Sons of Musa" ibn Shakir (fl. c. 830–870, Muhammad d. 873) · Baghdad · geometry, mechanics

    Three wealthy brothers who funded translations (they employed Thabit ibn Qurra, their greatest discovery) and did first-rate geometry of their own. Their Book on the Measurement of Plane and Spherical Figures treated areas and volumes in the manner of Archimedes but with a methodological novelty: they operated with the number \(\pi\) as a magnitude in its own right and presented Archimedean results in computational form. The book, translated by Gerard of Cremona as Verba filiorum, was a standard source for Archimedean geometry in Latin Europe and was used by Fibonacci and later by the 13th–14th century European geometers. Ahmad's Book of Ingenious Devices (automata, self-regulating mechanisms) belongs to the prehistory of control engineering.

    Sources: Berggren, Episodes; DSB; Clagett, Archimedes in the Middle Ages, vol. 1.

    Habash al-Hasib¶

    Ahmad ibn Abdallah Habash al-Hasib al-Marwazi ("Habash the Reckoner," fl. c. 825–870) · Baghdad, Samarra · astronomy, trigonometry

    A working astronomer of al-Ma'mun's observational program whose zijes helped naturalize trigonometric methods. He is credited with early systematic use of the shadow functions, the quantities we now call tangent and cotangent, in tabulated form, and with auxiliary tables that simplified spherical-astronomical computation. Attributions of "first use" in this area are contested among historians (Abu al-Wafa and others consolidated the six standard functions later), but Habash's tables mark the point where trigonometry in the Islamic world begins to outgrow its Greek chord-based inheritance.

    Sources: E. S. Kennedy, "A Survey of Islamic Astronomical Tables"; DSB.

    al-Mahani¶

    Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Isa al-Mahani (fl. c. 860, d. c. 880) · Baghdad · geometry, algebra

    Known for attempting to solve the Archimedean problem of dividing a sphere by a plane into segments with a given volume ratio (from On the Sphere and Cylinder II.4). Al-Mahani reduced it to a cubic equation, which he could not solve; the problem became famous in the tradition as "al-Mahani's equation" and was eventually handled by al-Khazin using conic sections. This episode began the research program, cubics via intersecting conics, that culminates in Omar Khayyam. He also wrote commentaries on Euclid Book X (irrational magnitudes), pushing the arithmetization of irrationals.

    Sources: Berggren, Episodes; DSB; Rashed.

    Thabit ibn Qurra¶

    Abu al-Hasan Thabit ibn Qurra al-Harrani (836–901) · Harran, then Baghdad · geometry, number theory, astronomy, mechanics

    A Sabian from Harran recruited by the Banu Musa, Thabit became the finest geometer of the ninth century and one of the great mathematicians of the entire tradition.

    Number theory. His theorem on amicable numbers gives a rule generating amicable pairs: if \(p = 3\cdot 2^{n-1} - 1\), \(q = 3\cdot 2^n - 1\), and \(r = 9\cdot 2^{2n-1} - 1\) are all prime for \(n > 1\), then \(2^n pq\) and \(2^n r\) are amicable. This is the first known general theorem producing amicable pairs; Fermat and Descartes rediscovered pairs derivable from it centuries later, and Euler generalized the rule.

    Geometry and proto-calculus. He computed the area of a parabolic segment and volumes of paraboloids by summation methods that partition intervals into unequal parts, work squarely in the lineage from Archimedes' method of exhaustion toward integration. He wrote one of the serious early attempts on the parallel postulate and a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem to arbitrary triangles.

    Translation and revision. He revised the Ishaq ibn Hunayn translations of Euclid and Ptolemy into the standard editions of the tradition, and translated or revised works of Archimedes and Apollonius, including Books V–VII of the Conics, which survive only in Arabic; without Thabit's school, a third of the Conics would be lost.

    Transmission. His treatise on the qarastun (steelyard balance) circulated in Latin as Liber karastonis; his astronomical and geometric works influenced both the Islamic and, via translation, the Latin traditions.

    Sources: Berggren, Episodes, chs. 2–3; Rashed; DSB (B. Rosenfeld and A. T. Grigorian, "Thabit ibn Qurra"); Roshdi Rashed (ed.), Thabit ibn Qurra: Science and Philosophy in Ninth-Century Baghdad (2009).

    Qusta ibn Luqa¶

    Qusta ibn Luqa al-Ba'labakki (c. 820 – c. 912) · Baalbek, Baghdad, Armenia · translation, mathematics, medicine

    A Greek Christian from Baalbek, fluent in Greek, who translated Diophantus's Arithmetica into Arabic (as "The Art of Algebra"), along with works of Theodosius, Autolycus, and Hero. The Arabic Diophantus is historically precious twice over: it shaped the number-theoretic algebra of al-Karaji and al-Samawal, and four of its books survive only in Qusta's Arabic version, discovered in manuscript in Mashhad and published in the 1970s–80s, doubling the extant Diophantus.

    Sources: J. Sesiano, Books IV to VII of Diophantus' Arithmetica in the Arabic Translation (1982); Rashed's edition; DSB.

    al-Battani¶

    Abu Abdallah Muhammad ibn Jabir al-Battani (c. 858–929) · Raqqa · astronomy, trigonometry

    Latinized as Albategnius. Though his career extends past 900, he belongs with the foundational generation. His Kitab al-Zij (the Sabian Tables) rests on some forty years of his own observations at Raqqa and is the most influential single zij of the tradition in Europe. Mathematically, al-Battani used the sine systematically where Ptolemy had used chords, employed the relation equivalent to \(\tan\theta\) via shadow lengths, and stated the formula equivalent to the spherical law of cosines for his qibla-type problems. He determined the solar eccentricity and the obliquity of the ecliptic with accuracy that stood for centuries and discovered the motion of the solar apogee.

    Transmission. Translated by Robert of Chester and, definitively, by Plato of Tivoli as De motu stellarum; printed in 1537. Copernicus cites al-Battani repeatedly in De revolutionibus, and his data was still used by Kepler and, for lunar acceleration studies, by Dunthorne in the eighteenth century.

    Sources: DSB (W. Hartner, "al-Battani"); Kennedy, Survey; the standard edition is C. A. Nallino, Al-Battani sive Albatenii Opus Astronomicum (1899–1907).
