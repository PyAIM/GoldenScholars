# The Translation Movement into Latin

## The Mirror Image

Between roughly 1100 and 1300, Latin Europe did to Arabic science exactly what Abbasid Baghdad had done to Greek science three centuries earlier: it translated systematically, hungrily, and with state and church patronage. The direction of the intellectual gradient is not in dispute; European scholars traveled to the frontier zones because that was where the books were.

## The Centers

**Toledo.** Reconquered by Castile in 1085 with its Arabic libraries intact, Toledo became the greatest translation center. **Gerard of Cremona** (c. 1114–1187) came seeking Ptolemy's *Almagest*, stayed a lifetime, and translated (with a team, including the Mozarab Galib) over seventy works: the *Almagest* itself, Euclid's *Elements* (from Arabic), [al-Khwarizmi](../scholars/era-01-foundations.md#al-khwarizmi)'s *Algebra*, [al-Nayrizi](../scholars/era-02-tenth-century.md#al-nayrizi)'s Euclid commentary, the [Banu Musa](../scholars/era-01-foundations.md#the-banu-musa)'s geometry (*Verba filiorum*), [Jabir ibn Aflah](../scholars/era-04-twelfth-century.md#jabir-ibn-aflah)'s *Islah* (spherical trigonometry), al-Farghani's astronomy, and much of the medical corpus. His students' memorial list of his translations is a foundational document of European science.

**The Ebro valley and the north of Spain.** **Robert of Chester** translated al-Khwarizmi's *Algebra* at Segovia in **1145**, the year Latin algebra begins, and, with Hermann of Carinthia, worked on astronomical and religious texts. **Plato of Tivoli** translated [al-Battani](../scholars/era-01-foundations.md#al-battani); **Hugh of Santalla**, **John of Seville**, and others fed the stream.

**England via Spain.** **Adelard of Bath** (fl. 1116–1142) translated Euclid's *Elements* from Arabic (the version that dominated Latin geometry teaching for centuries) and [al-Khwarizmi's zij](../scholars/era-02-tenth-century.md#maslama-al-majriti) in Maslama's Córdoban recension, Latin Europe's first real astronomical tables with instructions.

**Sicily and Italy.** The trilingual Norman court (Arabic, Greek, Latin) produced translations directly from Greek as well as Arabic; later, the Alfonsine court of Castile (1250s–80s) produced the Alfonsine Tables in the Arabic zij tradition and Castilian translations of Arabic astronomy.

## What Was Translated, and What Was Not

Europe received: al-Khwarizmi (algebra and arithmetic), Abu Kamil, al-Farghani, al-Battani, al-Zarqali/Toledan Tables, Jabir ibn Aflah, Ibn al-Haytham's *Optics*, al-Nayrizi, the Banu Musa, Thabit (partially), al-Kindi (partially), Maslama's Ptolemy material, and the philosophical corpus (Avicenna, Averroes) whose mathematical sections traveled with it.

Europe did **not** receive (in this period): [al-Biruni](../scholars/era-03-golden-age.md#al-biruni), [al-Karaji](../scholars/era-02-tenth-century.md#al-karaji), [al-Samawal](../scholars/era-04-twelfth-century.md#al-samawal), [Khayyam](../scholars/era-03-golden-age.md#omar-khayyam)'s algebra, [Sharaf al-Din al-Tusi](../scholars/era-04-twelfth-century.md#sharaf-al-din-al-tusi), [al-Kashi](../scholars/era-07-samarkand.md#ghiyath-al-din-jamshid-al-kashi), the Maghrebi symbolists, or (directly) the Maragha planetary corpus. The ceiling of the tradition was far above what crossed the Pyrenees, which is why the "Islamic mathematics = preserved Greek books" cliché fails: what Europe missed was precisely the most original material, much of it recovered only by modern historians in manuscript.

## The Word-Level Evidence

The translation movement is fossilized in vocabulary: *algebra*, *algorithm*, *cipher/zero*, *sine* (by mistranslation), *zenith*, *nadir*, *azimuth*, *almanac*, *alidade*, and dozens of star names. See [Etymology](../legacy/etymology.md).

## Assessment

Charles Burnett, the leading modern student of the movement, describes twelfth-century translation as the transfusion that made the European university curriculum possible: the quadrivium got its advanced textbooks (Euclid, Ptolemy, al-Farghani, the algorismus) almost entirely through Arabic. When Europe's own creative mathematics begins, with [Fibonacci](fibonacci-europe.md), it begins explicitly as a continuation of Arabic mathematics, learned in Arabic, from Arabic teachers, in North Africa.
