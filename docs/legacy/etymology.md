# Etymology of Mathematical Terms

Ordinary technical vocabulary preserves the transmission history like tree rings. Each entry below gives the standard etymology; where an etymology is folk or disputed, that is said.

## algebra
From *al-jabr* ("the restoring/completion"), the first operation in the title of [al-Khwarizmi](../scholars/era-01-foundations.md#al-khwarizmi)'s treatise *al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wa-l-muqabala*. In medieval Spanish and Italian, an *algebrista* could also mean a bone-setter, one who restores what is broken, the same Arabic sense applied to fractures. The mathematical meaning won.

## algorithm / algorism
From **Algorismi/Algoritmi**, the Latinization of al-Khwarizmi's name in the translations of his arithmetic (*Dixit Algorizmi*). Medieval *algorism* = computation with Hindu-Arabic numerals; the modern spelling and generalized sense (any effective procedure) were influenced by false association with Greek *arithmos* (number). A ninth-century Baghdad civil servant's hometown surname (Khwarazm, the region south of the Aral Sea) is now the central noun of computer science.

## cipher, zero
Both from Arabic *sifr* ("empty"), translating Sanskrit *sunya* (the empty place in positional notation). Latin *cifra/cephirum* split into two words: Italian *zefiro* → *zero* (the digit), and *cipher* (first meaning the zero, then any numeral, then secret writing, the last sense probably because encoded text looked like meaningless numerals). One Arabic word, two fundamental modern terms in mathematics and cryptography.

## sine
The famous mistranslation. Sanskrit *jya/jiva* (bow-string, the half-chord) was transliterated into Arabic as *j-y-b*; read as the genuine Arabic word *jayb* (fold, pocket, bosom of a garment), it was translated into Latin as *sinus* (fold, bay). See [Trigonometry](../themes/trigonometry.md). The other function names are Latin add-ons: *cosinus* (complement's sine), *tangens* and *secans* (touching and cutting lines, introduced by Thomas Fincke, 1583), but tangent and cotangent as **functions** originate as the Islamic shadow functions (*zill*, shadow).

## zenith
From Arabic *samt al-ra's* ("direction of the head," the point overhead). In manuscript Latin, *samt* was miscopied, an *m* read as *ni*, yielding *senit/cenit* → zenith. A scribal error is permanently installed in every astronomy textbook.

## nadir
From *nazir* ("opposite"), as in *nazir al-samt*, the point opposite the zenith. Clean transmission, no scribal accident this time.

## azimuth
From *al-sumut*, plural of the same *samt* (direction): the compass bearings along the horizon.

## almanac
From Arabic *al-manakh*, attested in al-Andalus for astronomical calendars/tables; the word's own deeper origin is uncertain (this is the standard but debated etymology, flagged as such in the OED).

## almagest
Ptolemy's *Syntaxis* became, in the Islamic world, *al-majisti* (from Greek *megiste*, "the greatest"), and returned to Europe as *Almagest*, a Greek superlative wearing an Arabic article, the whole transmission story in a single word.

## Other survivals
*Alidade* (the sighting rule of an astrolabe, *al-idada*); *alcohol* and *alkali* and *alchemy/chemistry* from the allied sciences; *nucha* (anatomy) and a hundred medical terms; and the star names: **Aldebaran** (al-dabaran, "the follower"), **Altair** (al-ta'ir, "the flying [eagle]"), **Betelgeuse** (yad al-jawza, "the hand of Orion," mangled in transliteration), **Deneb** (dhanab, "tail"), **Rigel** (rijl, "foot"), **Vega** (from al-nasr al-waqi, "the swooping eagle"), **Fomalhaut** (fam al-hut, "the fish's mouth"), **Algol** (ra's al-ghul, "the demon's head"). Roughly two hundred named stars carry Arabic names, most fixed in European usage through Latin translations of Arabic astronomical tables.

## And the letter x?
The claim that the algebraic **x** descends from Arabic *shay* ("thing," the unknown), via Old Spanish transcription with *x* (then pronounced "sh"), is attractive and repeated widely; the sober assessment is that direct documentary proof is lacking, and Descartes' typographic conventions in the *Géométrie* (1637) are the proximate source of x-y-z for unknowns. This reference reports the *shay* story as **plausible but unproven**.

*Sources: OED etymologies; Katz; Berggren; Kunitzsch, The Arabs and the Stars (star-name scholarship); standard histories of mathematical notation (Cajori).*
